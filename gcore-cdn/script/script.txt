## distance | distance

## light | light

## handshake | handshake
And one trip is never enough. First, your browser and the server [hs]shake hands: [tcp]open a connection, [tls]agree on encryption, then [get]ask for the page. Three or four round trips, and that's [blank]over half a *second* of blank screen.

## patience | patience
And people don't wait. Google found that [g53]more than half of mobile visitors leave if a page takes longer than [s3]three seconds. And Amazon calculated that [amz]every extra hundred milliseconds cost them [pct1]one percent of sales.

## edge | edge
Light can't go any faster. So you move the website [closer]closer. That's what a *CDN* does: a content delivery network. Gcore has [pops]more than two hundred and ten points of presence on [six]six continents, each keeping copies of websites right next to their users. For you in Frankfurt, the store is now [near]just a few kilometers away.

## routing | routing
But how does your browser find the nearest one? [same]Every point of presence answers to the same address, and the internet's routing simply [door]sends you to the closest door. That's called [anycast]*anycast*.

## cache | cache
The first visitor in Frankfurt gets a [miss]*miss*: the edge fetches the page from California once, [keep]and keeps it. Everyone after that gets a [hit]*hit*, straight from next door. On average, Gcore answers [p85]eighty-five percent of requests without even bothering the original server.

## weight | weight
On the way, the edge also puts the site [diet]on a diet. Photos get converted to [webp]WebP and AVIF, [lighter]up to eighty-five percent lighter. Fewer bytes, [less]less waiting.

## rush | rush

A CDN [split]splits the crowd across hundreds of locations, and each one serves its own neighborhood. Gcore grew up on exactly this. In fact, [g]the G in Gcore originally stood for [gaming]*gaming*.

## shield | shield

## result | result
