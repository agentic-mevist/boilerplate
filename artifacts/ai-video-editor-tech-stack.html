Rent the brain, own the timeline

Yes, the tools you named belong to different categories. Remotion, HyperFrames and Manim are rendering engines, the paint and brushes that turn code into video frames. video-use and OpenMontage are recipe books for Claude Code. They are mostly prompt files plus thin Python scripts around FFmpeg, transcription and generation APIs, with no proprietary model and almost no algorithm a strong developer couldn't rebuild. ChatCut is a full editor product. Its AI is rented (an LLM agent plus third-party generation models), but its multi-track timeline, rendering and export are real engineering. When Claude Code or Codex "edits a video", the model never watches anything. Speech-to-text turns the footage into a timestamped transcript, the model reads that text and a few still frames, writes a cut list, and FFmpeg does the cutting. Of the roughly 16 stages in that process, about ten need no LLM, a few need a cheap small model, and only two clearly benefit from a frontier model like Opus: deciding the story and pacing, and writing motion-graphics code. A well-designed pipeline costs roughly $0.15–0.50 per 10-minute video, against about $3 for an open-ended Opus agent loop and up to $41 per run in benchmarks (estimates; see below). Even the best agents still fail about three in four professional editing tasks, and they fail on taste rather than mechanics (Timeline-Bench). Rendering is mostly ordinary CPU work. Sandboxes are fine for drafts but poor as a render farm, and the clearest room for innovation is to stop re-encoding the whole video after every small edit. Fine-tuning a cheap model like Kimi only pays off once you own proprietary edit-preference data. Once your pipeline is efficient, the LLM is no longer your biggest cost. And the subsidized Claude Code subscription cannot legally power your product. The defensible moats are, in order: distribution, proprietary data on how users accept or reject edits, and owning the timeline where the work lives. Which model you use is not a moat.

Part 1 How it works

Five layers: the tools you named live on different floors

Think of an AI video editor as a restaurant. The stove and pans turn raw ingredients into a dish: that is FFmpeg, Remotion, HyperFrames and Manim, which turn instructions into pixels. The instruments tell the cook what's in the fridge: speech-to-text, silence detectors, face trackers and video-understanding models. The recipe book tells a cook which steps to follow and which mistakes to avoid: that is video-use, OpenMontage and the "skills" files that Remotion and HyperFrames ship for agents. The chef reads the recipes, decides what to make and works the stove: Claude Code, Codex, or an agent built into a product. The restaurant is everything a customer touches: the dining room (a timeline you can scrub and tweak), reservations (accounts and storage), the bill (credits and subscriptions) and the reviews (collaboration and approval). Claude Code is a talented freelance chef who can walk into any kitchen. video-use is a laminated recipe card. OpenMontage is a giant cookbook with a drawer full of supplier phone numbers. ChatCut is a restaurant that has its own chef but also lets you bring yours.

This layering comes straight from what the code does. Both agent kits explicitly hand animation work down to the engines. video-use tells the agent to "pick the engine per animation slot… HyperFrames / Remotion / Manim / PIL", and OpenMontage locks a project to Remotion or HyperFrames at proposal time (video-use; OpenMontage). The newer editor products Kino and OpenChatCut are themselves built on Remotion (Kino Launch HN; OpenChatCut). The trend that brought you here is real but developer-shaped. Monthly npm downloads of remotion rose from 569K in Dec 2025 to 10.0M in Oct 2026 after it shipped agent skills (npm API). Google searches for "claude video editing" are still below reporting thresholds, while "capcut" draws about a million a month (DataForSEO pulls cited in our earlier report).

OpenMontage and video-use are recipe books; ChatCut sells the kitchen

video-use, in very simple words, is a cheat sheet plus a small toolbox for Claude Code. You drop footage in a folder, chat with Claude Code, and get final.mp4 back (README). Inside are a 342-line instruction file (SKILL.md) and about 2,100 lines of Python that call FFmpeg, an image library, an audio library and one paid speech-to-text API, ElevenLabs Scribe. It has no model of its own and no secret algorithm. Its value is three good ideas and a list of hard-won rules. The first idea is a compact "packed transcript": words are grouped into phrases at every pause of half a second or more, so an hour of takes fits in about 12 KB of text, "a tenth the tokens of raw Scribe JSON." The second is an on-demand filmstrip-plus-waveform picture the agent looks at only at decision points, instead of drowning in frames. The README frames the choice as "30,000 frames × 1,500 tokens = 45M tokens of noise" versus "12KB text + a handful of PNGs." The third is a plan, confirm, render, self-check loop with a "critic sub-agent" briefed "to roast, not to praise." The rules are the kind of thing you only learn by shipping: 30 ms audio fades at every cut to avoid pops, subtitles burned in last, padding cuts 30–200 ms because "Scribe timestamps drift 50–100ms", and never cutting inside a word (video-use SKILL.md). It is deliberately anti-algorithm. One listed anti-pattern is "Hand-tuned moment-scoring functions. The LLM picks better than any heuristic you'll write." It is MIT-licensed, has about 30.7K stars, and looks like the top of a sales funnel: a hosted "video-use cloud" waitlist is already live.

OpenMontage is a much bigger cookbook aimed at making new videos, not cutting yours. Its README says it outright: "There is no code orchestrator. Your AI coding assistant IS the orchestrator" (OpenMontage). It ships 12 production "pipelines" written as YAML checklists (explainer, talking head, documentary montage, podcast repurpose, localization and more). Each one walks the agent through research → proposal → script → scene plan → assets → edit → compose, guided by hundreds of markdown "director" skills and about 100 Python wrappers around 60+ providers for voice, music, stock footage and video generation. It contains more real code than video-use: roughly 152K lines of Python and 219K lines of markdown and YAML, much of it vendored third-party skill packs. It also has some genuine heuristics. A provider picker scores options on seven hand-weighted dimensions (task fit 30%, quality 20%, control 15%, and so on). A "slideshow risk" scorer fails a scene plan that looks like a static slideshow. A CLIP-embedding index makes free archival footage searchable by meaning. Rounding it out are a cost tracker with budget caps and a local "Backlot" storyboard web page. None of these weights has been evaluated anywhere public. They look hand-picked, not learned. Its showcase claims a 60-second animated short for "$1.33" in generation costs. Its license is AGPL-3.0, which matters if you build a hosted product on it: the network-use clause would oblige you to publish your source.

ChatCut is not "just an interface", but the AI part of it is. It is a closed-source, real multi-track timeline editor (web, Mac and Windows) where "an AI agent changes a real multi-track timeline, and you can keep editing that timeline by hand." It renders locally up to 4K or in the cloud up to 1080p, and exports Premiere/DaVinci XML and editable CapCut/Jianying drafts (ChatCut Product Facts). Its intelligence is rented. Credits ($25 for 100, so $0.25 per credit) pay for third-party models such as Seedance, Kling, MiniMax, GPT Image and ElevenLabs, priced "based on the provider's cost for the selected model." Which LLM powers its built-in agent is not disclosed. Its public GitHub repo is only an agent plugin: about 41K lines of markdown skills plus a pointer to a hosted MCP server (agent-plugin). Notably, when you drive ChatCut from Claude Code, "the agent's own reasoning uses that agent's subscription", so ChatCut charges only for what Claude can't do. The company raised a $1.35M seed led by ZhenFund with Antler (Antler). Its claim of "400,000+ creators" is self-reported and unverified (chatcut.io). The best measure of how hard the "interface" really is comes from an unaffiliated open-source clone, OpenChatCut. Electron + React + Remotion, local Whisper, multicam, reframing and scene detection add up to about 294K lines of TypeScript (OpenChatCut).

What do these give you that plain Claude Code can't do out of the box? For video-use and OpenMontage the honest answer is nothing it can't do, only things it would do slowly and with bugs. Claude Code with FFmpeg and a word-level speech-to-text API can already transcribe, pack the transcript, cut silences and fillers, add fades and captions, grade, normalize loudness, check its own output, and spawn sub-agents to write Remotion overlays. The kits front-load tuned prompts so each session doesn't re-learn them. What plain Claude Code genuinely cannot give you is the product layer. Kino's founder put it precisely: "Opus 5.5 can edit videos and make great motion graphics now. But with a raw agent harness, you need to describe tiny edits either in words or in quick one-off UIs… you can't refer to footage shared across your devices, and feedback from teammates comes as screenshots on Slack" (Launch HN). The product layer is a frame-accurate timeline that a human can nudge, persistent project state, proxies and pro codecs, render infrastructure, review links, NLE round-trips and billing. That is what ChatCut, Kino and Descript sell, and it's what you'd be building.

Claude never watches your video; it reads it and writes a cut list

The process in plain words

When people say "Claude Code edited my video", here is what actually happened. First, a script asks FFmpeg for the video's basic facts (length, frame rate, resolution) and pulls out the audio. Second, a specialist speech model (not Claude) writes down every word with its start and end time, down to tenths of a second. Third, more small tools mark where the silences are, who is speaking, and where the camera cuts. Fourth, Claude reads that transcript like a script, sometimes looks at a few "contact sheet" images (a grid of still frames), and decides what to keep. Its output is a cut list: a table of start time, end time and reason. Fifth, FFmpeg cuts the original file at those times, glues the pieces, adds fades, captions and loudness correction, and writes a new file. Sixth, a good setup re-checks the result by re-transcribing it or grabbing frames at every cut. Codex, OpenAI's coding agent, works the same way. Benchmark authors note that GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5 "accept only text and images, so they read contact sheets of one frame per second", whereas Gemini 3.8 Flash "watches the edit with sound" (Timeline-Bench). One widely shared Claude Code workflow says it plainly: the TSV cut list "is the entire edit plan", and "adjusting a number here is much faster than re-editing a video project" (ChatPRD). This text-first approach isn't new. It descends from Adobe and Berkeley's 2012 work linking "a text transcript of the video to the corresponding locations in the raw footage" (Berthouzoz et al.), which is the idea behind Descript.

The pattern is that you need a big model only where taste or open-ended code is involved. Cutting silences, removing fillers, normalizing loudness, reframing and rendering involve no judgment once the speech model has produced timestamped words. Removing "um" is literally deleting rows from a list. You do not need Opus for cutting and simple steps. A small model is enough for classification-like jobs: keep or drop this sentence, pick the cleaner of two takes, title this chapter, turn "make it snappier" into concrete edits. Anthropic markets Haiku 5.5 for "classification, extraction, and routing" (Anthropic pricing). The frontier model earns its price in two places. The first is deciding what the story is in long or messy footage. The second is writing novel motion-graphics code, where cheaper coding models produce more broken renders. On Timeline-Bench, frontier stacks resolved 18–27% of tasks against 4–11% for flash and open models. Weaker agents shipped content defects (audio glitches, repeated footage) in 19% of rejections versus 10% for the top six (Timeline-Bench). An important caveat: no benchmark isolates the planning step alone. Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5 haven't been tested on these leaderboards, and the best Claude result was Opus 5 in Claude Code, not 5.5. Whether Sonnet plans as well as Opus on your footage is something you have to measure with your own evaluation set.

There is a better way than "let Opus figure it out"

An open-ended agent loop re-discovers the same recipe on every video. Timeline-Bench measured what that costs. Agents spent "98% of their perception" before the first render, which arrived "72–90% of the way through a run." Median runtime was 16–61 minutes per run, and runtime was unrelated to success (ρ = 0.15). Agents "check their renders for defects, not craft" and "claim success in 93% of runs" (Timeline-Bench). Of 771 failed runs, 562 passed every technical test and failed only on quality. Agents cut at 0.72× the pace of the human reference, so their edits were too slow. The harness matters as much as the model. AgenticVBench found the same GPT-5.5 scoring 38%, 37% and 18% on assembly tasks depending only on which agent harness ran it (AgenticVBench). Adding "curated editorial guidance" lifted Timeline-Bench's best agent from 21.4% to 26.8%. Better instructions moved the needle as much as switching between top models.

The better design is a pipeline with a bounded LLM core. Run all the cheap analyzers in parallel to build a "footage dossier": transcript with word times, silences, speakers, shots, faces and short visual captions. Make one planning call that returns a strict, validated edit plan. Render deterministically, then verify automatically by re-transcribing the output to confirm the removed words are gone, checking frames at each cut and measuring loudness. Allow at most one or two critic-driven revisions, the Editor-plus-Critic pattern that EditDuet showed "vastly outperforms existing approaches" on human preference (EditDuet). Keep a human approval checkpoint on the plan, as both LAVE's plan-approve-execute design and the practitioner TSV workflow do (LAVE). Reserve the open-ended agent mode for motion graphics and unusual requests. One honest gap: no controlled study compares a fixed pipeline with an agent loop on the same tasks. This recommendation is inferred from the benchmark failure analyses and general agent-cost evidence (Aerospike).

Rendering is mostly cheap CPU work, and the waste is re-encoding everything

FFmpeg in plain words

A video file is like a sealed envelope holding two zipped flipbooks, one of pictures and one of sound. FFmpeg works in five steps. It opens the envelope and separates the tracks (demux). It unzips the pictures into full images (decode). It draws on them: crops, overlays, captions, color (filter). It re-zips them (encode). It seals a new envelope (mux) (ffmpeg.org). The zipping trick is that only some pages are full pictures (keyframes). The pages in between store only what changed since the last one. By default x264 puts a keyframe every 250 frames, which is one every 10 seconds at 25 fps (FFmpeg wiki: Seeking). If you change nothing about the pictures, FFmpeg can move the zipped pages without unzipping (-c copy, "stream copy"). That is nearly instant, but it can only cut cleanly at a keyframe: "There is no way to precisely cut a video with FFmpeg if you need to cut outside an I-frame without re-encoding" (Video StackExchange). Anything that changes pixels, such as captions, zooms, overlays or a frame-exact cut, forces the unzip-draw-rezip cycle. That cycle is where all the CPU or GPU time goes. The quality knob for H.264 is CRF: 23 is the default, 17–18 is "visually lossless or nearly so", and +6 roughly halves file size (FFmpeg wiki: H.264). A middle path called "smart cut" re-encodes only the few seconds around each cut and stream-copies the rest (dev.to).

How the code-based engines render

Remotion and HyperFrames both work like a screenshot loop. They load a web page in headless Chrome, move it to frame N, photograph it, and pipe the photos to FFmpeg. "The renderer seeks each frame in headless Chrome and encodes the result with FFmpeg", and HyperFrames calls itself "inspired by Remotion", with plain HTML instead of React as the main difference (HyperFrames README). HyperFrames computes each frame's time with integer math rather than a clock, which makes output deterministic: the same input always yields the same frames (HyperFrames determinism). Every frame costs a full browser paint, so these engines are great for graphics and poor value for re-encoding a talking head. Remotion's own numbers show the spread on Lambda. A 10-minute HD video rendered in about 56–61 s for $0.10 (about 10× realtime, $0.01 per minute). A 10-second 4K clip took 45–53 s, slower than realtime (Remotion cost example). Licensing differs in ways that matter for a product. Remotion is free for companies of up to 3 people. Above that, "Remotion for Automators" costs $0.01 per render with a $100/month minimum (Remotion pricing). HyperFrames is Apache-2.0 "with no per-render fees". Revideo is MIT (Revideo). Manim's default CPU renderer can take "minutes or even hours" per animation, and the OpenGL renderer is much faster (slama.dev).

The sensible split, which Kapwing's architecture also reflects, is cuts and concatenation in FFmpeg and browser rendering only for the overlay layers. That means titles, captions and cards, rendered with transparency and composited on top. Kapwing uses the concat demuxer "which does not require re-encoding" to "significantly reduce the total processing", and draws subtitles as PNG overlays (Kapwing engineering).

What hardware you actually need

A 10-minute 1080p talking-head edit is a light job. Encoding is "compute intensive but also highly parallelizable", so more cores means proportionally faster encodes. Kapwing, which processes over 100K projects a day, finds GPUs "of minimal utility to most video encoders" and keeps a GPU cluster only for jobs like background removal (Kapwing).

The bottleneck in a sandbox is rarely the encode. It is moving gigabytes in and out. A 3 GB source takes about 4 minutes to upload at 100 Mbps, which is longer than the encode itself (estimate). Rendi prices by GB processed for exactly this reason. Production SaaS splits the work. Kapwing renders server-side on autoscaled GCP FFmpeg clusters, using low-res proxies for preview while "the files used to process a user's final video will always be the original high-quality inputs" (Kapwing). Descript moved decode, composite and encode into the browser, kept a server-side "Media Transform Server" to normalize uploads, and calls streaming cloud-GPU rendering "perhaps the most expensive approach" (Descript). The recommendation: keep footage in object storage (S3 or R2). Let the agent's sandbox work only on a lightweight edit description and low-res proxies. Send final renders to a dedicated backend that pulls originals directly from storage, or to the user's own browser or Mac.

Where the room for innovation is

The render layer is still largely "photograph every frame, then re-encode everything", and AI editing makes that waste worse, because agents produce many near-identical drafts. Six opportunities stand out, all inferred from the gaps above. Incremental re-rendering keeps the last output in fixed-length segments, maps each edit to the segments it touches, re-encodes only those, and stream-copies the rest. Remotion already chunks renders on Lambda, but "seamless concatenation of chunks is not a public API" (Remotion). No published product offering this for programmatic editors turned up, so it is either open territory or a gap in our search. A content-addressed render cache hashes the composition, props, frame range and renderer version so unchanged chunks are never re-rendered. HyperFrames' determinism, "same input, same frames, same output", makes this safe. Overlay-only HTML rendering composites transparent graphics over stream-copied footage. Two-tier rendering pairs instant 540p proxy previews with high-quality finals. An agent-native render API accepts a timeline rather than raw FFmpeg strings and returns proof: thumbnails at each cut, loudness stats, keyframe warnings, and a re-transcription diff confirming that the removed words are gone. Existing FFmpeg-as-a-service products sell only raw command execution. Cost-aware routing tries stream copy first, then a CPU re-encode, then a hardware encoder, then distributed fan-out. Of these, the verification API and incremental rendering most directly address what benchmarks say agents lack: a cheap way to iterate many times on craft.

Part 2 Building a product

Fine-tuning pays for taste and latency, not tokens

What "fine-tuning for video editing" would actually mean

The phrase hides three different projects. Pixel models change what is inside the frame: Runway Aleph retexturing a scene, Mirage's generative models, academic instruction-editing models trained on millions of video pairs. These are foundation-model efforts. Leave them to Runway, Mirage and ByteDance and call them by API (Aleph costs 28 credits per second of output (Runway API)). Perception models find the good bits, the hooks and the dead air. This is well-trodden research with public datasets such as QVHighlights (~10K videos) and recent RL-tuned temporal grounding like TempSamp-R1 (QVHighlights; TempSamp-R1). A small fine-tuned vision model here is cheap to run per minute and plausible for a startup. Edit-decision models choose what to cut, keep, reorder, caption and zoom. This is where a fine-tuned LLM could beat frontier models on taste, and it is where the data does not exist publicly: no large open corpus of raw-footage-to-final-cut decisions turned up.

Is video editing inherently agentic with no single best model?

Partly. The mechanical stages aren't agentic at all; they're a fixed pipeline. The creative core (story, pacing, graphics) is open-ended and benefits from an agent that can try something, look and revise. Among models there is genuinely no single winner. On Timeline-Bench the top stacks, GPT-6 Astra in Codex (26.8%, with editorial guidance) and Claude Opus 5 in Claude Code (23.2%), fall within roughly ±11-point confidence intervals and "cannot be told apart." Human editors still preferred the human reference edit 83.5% of the time (Timeline-Bench). On AgenticVBench the best stack scored 38.4% against humans' 88.5% (AgenticVBench). The practical conclusion is to treat the model as a swappable part and route per stage. Your durable assets are the harness, the evaluation set and the data.

The Claude Code subsidy subsidizes your competitors, not you

Claude Code subscriptions really are cheap relative to API list prices. Upbound measured its engineers' usage at 13× the seat price on average, 7× for the median and 52× for the heaviest (Upbound). SemiAnalysis calls the plans "highly subsidized" (SemiAnalysis). A counter-view holds that list price isn't Anthropic's cost: open models of similar size serve at ~10% of Anthropic's prices, so the real subsidy is smaller (Martin Alderson). All of these multiples are third-party estimates against list prices. Anthropic publishes no cost-per-subscriber figure. The decisive fact for you is legal, not economic. Anthropic "does not permit third-party developers to offer Claude.ai login into their own applications, or to route requests through Free, Pro, Max, or Ultra plan credentials on behalf of their users", and products built on the Agent SDK "should use API key authentication" (Claude Code legal & compliance). Since Oct 7, 2026, Max, Ultra and Team plans include monthly API credits ($100 Max, $200 Ultra, pooled Team credits) that cover the API and Agent SDK but not Claude Code (Claude Platform docs). Your product pays API or open-model rates. Meanwhile, a creator with a $200 Max plan can run video-use for free. The subsidy lowers the price of your strongest competitor: DIY Claude Code.

There is one legal way to put the subsidy on your side: be a tool inside the user's agent. Ship an MCP server and skill so the user's own Claude Code pays for the reasoning, and charge for what Claude can't do, like rendering, generation, storage, review links and publishing. ChatCut does exactly this ("the agent's own reasoning uses that agent's subscription"), and OpusClip 4.0 shipped an MCP server and skill so outside agents can call it (OpusClip changelog). The trade-off is that you become a component in someone else's product.

Moats rank distribution and edit data above models

The market is crowded and moving fast. HeyGen reports $200M ARR (HeyGen), Synthesia is valued at $4B (CNBC), and OpusClip is at "$50M+ ARR" per its CEO (arr.club). Tracxn counts 48 new "automated video editing" startups founded in 2026 (Tracxn). Platforms are absorbing the generic use case. If I were building this

I would not build "a general AI video editor." CapCut, Adobe-plus-Claude, Descript and OpusClip already cover generic "prompt → rough cut." I would pick one vertical with specific taste and a measurable outcome, such as long-form talking head → tightened cut plus shorts for B2B founders and coaches, podcast → clips, or e-commerce ads where conversion data supplies the reward signal. I would own the timeline and the data, rent the brain, and let users bring their own agent. Your existing audience, which our earlier research identified as the "honest, analytical" voice in a hype-heavy niche, is the rank-1 moat most founders lack.

A 3-step starting plan:

  1. Weeks 1–4: build the pipeline as a CLI and an evaluation set before any UI. Use video-use (MIT) as the reference design. Run hosted speech-to-text (Scribe or Deepgram) or self-hosted Parakeet, the deterministic analyzers, one Sonnet planning call into a strict JSON edit plan, an FFmpeg render with the 30 ms fade and padding rules, and automatic re-transcription QA. Run it on 50 real videos from 10 creators in your chosen vertical. Have them mark every cut as accepted or rejected, and record time saved. Run the same set through Haiku, Sonnet and Opus to settle the "do I need Opus" question with your own numbers. Expected cost: tens of dollars in API fees.
  2. Months 2–4: ship the review surface and the agent door at the same time. Build a transcript-plus-timeline editor where every AI decision is visible and adjustable by hand, with browser export by default. Ship an MCP server and skill so Claude Code and Codex users can drive it on their own subscriptions. Price per source minute, metering generation separately. Log every accept, reject, undo and trim adjustment from the first user. That log is the asset.
  3. Months 4–9: turn usage into a flywheel. Train a lightweight reward model on accept/reject and re-edit data. Use it to rank candidate edits, then route more stages to cheap models where your evaluation set shows no loss. Add incremental and cached rendering for fast iteration. Only once you hold tens of thousands of graded edits, and LLM spend or latency actually hurts, distill or RL-tune an open model (Kimi or Qwen via Tinker) on your harness.

Open questions for you to answer before building:

  1. Who is the customer: solo creators (price-sensitive, viral growth), professional editors (want NLE round-trips, not replacement), or brands and agencies (pay for consistency and approvals)?
  2. Which single vertical has the clearest taste signal and outcome data you can access, such as retention, conversion or the client's approval?
  3. Do you want to be the app people open, or the best tool inside their Claude Code? The MCP-first route is cheaper and rides the subsidy, but it cedes the relationship.
  4. Cut-your-own-footage or generate-new-video? They have very different cost structures: cents per edit versus about $7 per generated minute.
  5. Can you hire or partner with one strong video-infrastructure engineer (codecs, WebCodecs, FFmpeg)? The timeline and render layer is where the hard engineering lives.
  6. Will your users let you store raw footage and learn from their edits? Consent and licensing terms decide whether moat #2 exists.
  7. How will you use your channel: building in public as the distribution engine, or keeping the product separate from your content brand?

Conclusion

The useful mental shift is that "AI video editing" is not one AI problem. It is a cheap signal-processing pipeline wrapped around a small, expensive judgment core, sitting inside a hard-to-build editing product. The open-source kits that look like breakthroughs mostly package the pipeline knowledge, which is valuable but free. The frontier model supplies judgment that is still mediocre: about a quarter of professional tasks solved, and failures that pass every technical check. The product layer, meaning the timeline, rendering and collaboration, is where the engineering effort and switching costs live. That reframes the build-versus-depend question. You cannot escape dependence on frontier labs by fine-tuning early. You escape it by making the model a swappable part, so that whichever lab is ahead plugs in. Meanwhile you accumulate the one thing no lab has: a record of which cuts real people kept.

The second-order implication is about timing. Because agents are bad at taste and labs are improving them every few months, the window for a startup is not "out-model the labs." It is to own the feedback loop before the labs make generic editing good enough. A product that logs human corrections from its first user, verifies its own renders cheaply, and re-renders only what changed will get better and cheaper with every edit. A thin wrapper on Claude Code will get commoditized with every model release.
