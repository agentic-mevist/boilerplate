Research briefAI video editing with ClaudeCompiled Nov 7, 2026

Claude didn't kill editors. It killed scarcity.

Most of your thesis holds up, and the data makes it sharper. AI really has made production cheap enough to flood every channel. New App Store submissions rose 24% in 2025 and roughly doubled in the first half of 2026 (Appfigures, The Atlantic). About half of new web articles are now AI-written (Graphite). Total attention, meanwhile, is close to flat: US adults gain only 1–2 media minutes a day per year (eMarketer). So distribution and trust do become the bottleneck. "Editing with Claude" is a real trend, but it's a developer-shaped niche. Remotion's npm downloads went from about 0.57M a month in Dec 2025 to 10.0M in Oct 2026 (npm). Yet Claude-specific Google searches are still below Google's reporting threshold, while "capcut" gets about 1M a month. Anthropic has published no usage statistic on video editing. Claude also doesn't natively watch video: it reads transcripts and conducts ffmpeg. That's why it's strong at mechanical cuts and code-built motion graphics and weak at taste, which is exactly where human editors keep their value. Freelance demand backs this up: Fiverr reports human video editing up 36% alongside the AI boom (Fiverr). Two parts of your thesis need fixing. The faceless "pyramid scheme" claim is overstated: there are real earners, but the payout curve is brutal and the platforms are now policing it. And your plan is missing the biggest lever of all, packaging and retention. The opportunity for you is that dozens of creators made the "Claude killed video editors" tutorial, and almost nobody with reach made the honest, analytical version.

Cheap production floods supply while attention stays flat

The cleanest proof of your core thesis comes from software, not video, and that's useful on camera because it's an analogy viewers already feel. Apple's App Store received 557,000 new app submissions in 2025, up 24%. That reversed a seven-year decline, and Appfigures names vibe coding as a driver (Appfigures). In Q1 2026 new iOS apps were up 84% year over year (AppleInsider). The Atlantic reports nearly 1.3 million new iOS and Google Play apps in H1 2026, double 2025, with Tim Cook citing "more than 1,000 submissions an hour." Its companion segment was titled "Vibe-coded apps flood app stores, but no one uses them" (The Atlantic, seen in snippets only because the article was paywalled). GitHub shows the same curve: a record 121M new repos in 2025 (Octoverse 2025), then 17.9B pushes in 12 months, "more than 2024 and 2025 combined" (Octoverse 2026).

Content follows the same pattern. YouTube now reports over 20 million videos uploaded daily (YouTube Press). AI-written articles overtook human ones in November 2024 and have held at roughly 50/50 since (Graphite 2026). The most useful single fact for your argument is that those AI articles make up only 12–14% of Google Search results (Graphite). Being produced is not the same as being seen. That gap is "distribution matters more" in one number.

Demand has not kept pace. eMarketer projects US adults' total daily media time to grow by just 2 minutes in 2025 and 1 minute in 2026 (eMarketer). Even TikTok's per-user time fell 6.9% in 2025 (eMarketer). So "people watch more video" is true as a share shift, not as growth in total attention. YouTube hit a record 15.8% of all US TV viewing in August 2026, rising 6% in a month when total TV usage fell 4.4% (Nielsen). In the UK, video-sharing platforms grew from 43 to 51 minutes per person per day between 2022 and 2025, while linear TV lost 14 minutes in a single year (Ofcom). Shorts views nearly tripled to 200 billion a day (TheWrap) without total minutes moving. Short-form multiplies the number of slots competing for the same attention.

On what video is used for: business adoption has saturated at about 91% of companies using it for marketing. That edition of Wyzowl's survey also shows AI-tool use jumping from 51% to 63% while the share reporting good ROI dropped from 93% to 82% (Wyzowl via Digital Applied; primary: wyzowl.com, which blocked automated fetches). The sample is only 266 respondents, so call it a signal, not proof. Still, "more AI, lower returns per video" in one year is an excellent on-camera beat. Education is the highest-value use for creators: a 300-channel dataset puts median Education & Science RPM at $10.22, versus $2.30 across all niches and $0.33 for kids content (AIR Media-Tech). That's relevant because your course video sits in the high-RPM bucket.

Here's where to steelman the other side. Jevons paradox cuts both ways: cheaper editing also expands demand for editing (NPR Planet Money). The strongest form of your thesis is not "spend more on marketing," because there's no hard data on budget shifts and mobile cost-per-install is roughly flat (Business of Apps). It's Ben Thompson's Aggregation Theory: when supply is abundant, value moves to whoever owns demand (Stratechery). For a creator, that means owning an audience and its trust. Herbert Simon said it in 1971: "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention" (Oxford Reference).

Claude editing is real, but it's a developer-shaped niche

Anthropic has not published any statistic on video editing. The Economic Index reports cover coding, writing and occupations. The only explicit video mention is that work-related creative writing is "mostly short-form video scripts, screenwriting, and speeches" (Anthropic, Jun 2026). Anthropic's signal is strategic rather than statistical. On Apr 28, 2026 it launched Claude for Creative Work, with connectors for Adobe (50+ tools, including Premiere) and Blender, plus the line "Claude can't replace taste or imagination, but it can open up new ways of working" (Anthropic). One critic argued that at launch the Adobe connector worked "at Adobe Express level, not Photoshop or Premiere" (MindStudio). What Anthropic has disclosed is Claude Code's scale: over $2.5B run-rate revenue by Feb 2026 (Anthropic).

The best evidence the trend is real is the package-download curve. After Remotion launched Agent Skills on Jan 20, 2026 ("make videos just with Claude Code!" — Remotion on X), monthly downloads of the remotion package went from 569K (Dec 2025) to 10.0M (Oct 2026). HeyGen's agent-first hyperframes went from zero to 3.85M a month (npm API; counts include automated installs). The Remotion skill shows 765K installs on skills.sh (skills.sh). Agent-native video repos created in 2026 already have huge star counts: HyperFrames 80K, OpenMontage 78K, browser-use's video-use 31K (GitHub API, Nov 7, 2026).

Your "is this my bubble?" instinct is also right, though. On Google web search in the US, "claude video editing," "claude code video editing" and "remotion claude" all fall below Google Ads' reporting threshold. "ai video editing" sits at a flat ~3K a month, while "capcut" draws 823K–1.22M and "descript" 74K–90K (DataForSEO pulls, Sep 2026). YouTube search tells a different story: "claude video editing" was near zero before 2026, then spiked to 26 vs 84 for "ai video editing" in the week of Oct 25, 2026 (Google Trends via DataForSEO). The fair summary for camera: creator content about Claude editing is growing faster than mainstream demand for it, and that is exactly how a bubble looks from the inside. It's also an opening, because YouTube search demand is rising right now.

Most of what YouTube calls "editing with Claude" is really two things. The first is video-as-code generation: Remotion, HyperFrames or Manim building motion graphics, title cards and explainers. The second is mechanical rough cuts: filler and silence removal via ffmpeg. Narrative editing of real footage is barely touched. The tools fall into four layers:

Is there a "Pencil.dev for video"? Not yet. In design, Pencil.dev (now pen.dev) is "an agentic canvas" you drive with Claude (pen.dev). Figma responded with its own Design Agent on May 20, 2026 (Figma). Video is following the same script. Challengers explicitly use the framing: diffusionstudio's editor calls itself "The VS Code of video editing, built for agents," and HyperFrames says it is "Built for agents." Meanwhile Adobe and Blackmagic are wiring assistants and Claude into their timelines. Our read is that agent-native challengers have won the motion-graphics niche, while incumbents still own footage editing. Your video can frame this as an open race.

What happens to editors? Every demand signal points to transformation, not death. In H1 2026, Fiverr saw AI video ads up 63%, "matched with parallel demand for human editing talent, including video editing (+36%) and short-form video editing (+27%)." The same report has Claude Code specialists up 938% (Fiverr). Upwork saw AI video generation and editing demand up 329% (Upwork). Both came from press-release snippets because the pages blocked fetching. Ben Affleck's arc is the best story beat you have. On Joe Rogan in January 2026 he said AI writing "goes to the mean, to the average" and would be "a tool just like sort of visual effects" (JRE Clips, 1:00–1:52). Two months later Netflix bought his AI post-production startup InterPositive (Netflix) for $587M cash (Variety). He has since said he used AI "for lots of things" in his Netflix film Animals (Deadline). The skeptic became the seller, and both positions fit "AI is a tool, not a replacement."

Ali Abdaal is your other anchor, with one correction: he isn't a novice editor. He edited his own videos for about three years as a doctor. What was new to him was AI editing. His verdict after six hours: "about 97%, especially on the motion graphics front… didn't cost anything on top of my Claude subscription." It was "about 20 shotted," and "human plus AI is… the sweet spot right now" (YouTube, 45:02–45:54).

Faceless arbitrage pays a few, then gets policed

The "pyramid scheme" framing overstates it. Real ad-revenue earners exist. Fortune reviewed AdSense records for 22-year-old Adavia Davis, whose five faceless channels gross an estimated ~$700K a year at 85–89% margins. He uses Claude for scripts and visuals and ElevenLabs for narration. He also sells a course, and he expects the window to close around 2027 as big media companies copy the pipeline (Fortune). At the other end, Kapwing built an honest faceless channel with an AI script and the stock ElevenLabs "Adam" voice. It reached 1.1M views, 37 watch hours in 90 days and $0 revenue, and YouTube briefly removed it as spam (Kapwing). The accurate framing is a power-law plus an arbitrage window. Selling the dream (courses, pipelines, SaaS) is more stable than running the channels. It isn't the only way money gets made.

Be careful with the earnings figures you'll see quoted. Social Blade estimates apply a flat $0.25–$4.00 RPM, a 16x spread (Social Blade). The Infographics Show's yearly revenue is estimated anywhere from $104K to "$5M+" depending on who you ask. Every "this slop channel makes $4M a year" headline is a Social Blade midpoint (Kapwing).

The platforms are closing the window. YouTube's July 15, 2025 update made "repetitive or mass-produced" content explicitly non-monetizable (YouTube Help). In January 2026 Neal Mohan named "AI slop" a 2026 priority (YouTube). Sixteen of the 100 biggest slop channels were deleted or wiped that month (The Verge). A Google research system terminated 130,000 channels in 50,000 clusters by keying on "templated narratives" and shared automation. Commentators identify the platform as YouTube, but that is not officially confirmed (Fstoppers). The collateral damage is relevant to your own history. Human-made faceless channels report demonetization, and Kurzgesagt says YouTube's detector wrongly flagged its work as AI slop (TNW, Kurzgesagt on X). Data-race charts and templated chat formats like Grafels and Sync the Chat sit squarely in the "repetitive" category the policy targets.

Your sponsorship instinct is right at the aggregate level. Brand deals are about 70% of creator revenue (Goldman Sachs, 2023), US creator ad spend reached $37B in 2025, up 26% (IAB), and MrBeast earns more from chocolate than from YouTube (Bloomberg). The nuance: faceless channels do get CPM-priced direct-response sponsors (VPNs, apps). The big, long-term brand partnerships Samir Chaudry describes, worth "$500K, $1M, or $3M," go to personalities (LinkedIn). Treat any "face-on-camera creators earn 20–30% more" claim as vendor marketing.

On "it all looks the same," trust data is your strongest evidence. Consumer preference for AI-generated creator content fell from 60% in 2023 to 26% in 2025 (Billion Dollar Boy). Content merely suspected of being AI loses 48% of trust and 60% of emotional connection (Raptive). An identical post labeled "AI-generated" gets up to 46 points less emotional engagement (Copenhagen Business School). The penalty is triggered by perception. Anything that reads as AI pays the tax: a stock TTS voice, default Remotion motion, generic script cadence. Sameness is also literally the signal Google's spam system clusters on. So the moat is whatever is expensive to copy: a recognizable person and voice, a point of view, original data or testing, community, and owned products. Instagram's Adam Mosseri put it this way: "authenticity is becoming infinitely reproducible," so raw human signals gain value for now (Om Malik).

Packaging and audio decide performance; AI handles the mechanical middle

Your list of what makes social video good (hook, re-hooks, visuals, sound, value, B-roll, sound design) is missing the first item, packaging. MrBeast's production guide makes the point: "I Spent 50 Hours In Ketchup" versus "…In My Front Yard" is the same effort, but one is "easily 100x more viral." Its rule is "you must know the title and thumbnails of the videos you are making" before you make them (MrBeast guide, Simon Willison summary). Colin & Samir decide the idea, title and thumbnail before writing the three-act story (Musicbed).

Retention is measurable and specific. YouTube's "Intro" metric is the share of viewers still watching at 30 seconds. YouTube explicitly ties it to whether the opening "matched the viewer's expectation of the video's thumbnail and title" (YouTube Help). MrBeast's team lost 21M of 60M viewers in minute one and considered that above average. They plan re-engagements around minutes 3 and 6. YouTube's thumbnail A/B test picks winners by watch-time share, not CTR (YouTube Help), so clickbait that doesn't deliver loses the test.

Audio has the best science behind it. In two experiments, degraded audio made viewers rate the talk as worse, the speaker as "less intelligent and less likeable," and the research as less important (Newman & Schwarz 2018; USC summary). No comparable evidence exists that 4K beats 1080p. Captions matter because people often watch muted in public: 69% in public versus 25% in private (Forbes on Verizon/Publicis 2019). The B-roll-frequency and sound-design claims you planned to make are folklore. No rigorous public study isolates them, so present them as craft opinion, not data. Shot lengths in films did fall from about 12 s (1930) to about 2.5 s (2014) (Cutting et al.). Pacing should follow story, though, not just get faster.

Claude's strengths line up with the mechanical middle of this pipeline because of how it works. It does not watch video or hear audio. Claude accepts images, not video files. Every "Claude edited my video" setup is a pipeline: speech-to-text produces word timestamps, Claude reads the text and writes a cut list, and ffmpeg renders it. Pipelines like video-use add on-demand filmstrip and waveform images at decision points ("The LLM never watches the video. It reads it." — video-use; LaoZhang AI). Looking at frames is expensive. One 1080p frame costs about 2,700 tokens, so a 10-minute video at one frame per second is about 1.6M tokens, versus roughly 7.5–21K for its transcript (LaoZhang AI). Failures cluster where judgment lives. In one vendor test, Claude reported a 299-second highlight reel whose cuts actually summed to 578 seconds, and all 11 cuts broke mid-sentence (cutback.video; the vendor sells a competing product). A 16-skill bake-off found Remotion output looks "close to the default" without a design direction (awesome-claude-video-skills). That default look is the technical root of the sameness problem.

By format, Claude Code fits speech-driven content best: talking heads, narrated tutorials, podcast clips. It's weakest where the edit is driven by visuals: vlogs, multicam, action, B-roll chosen by look (LaoZhang AI). For "what happens visually," people route clips to Gemini, which accepts video at roughly 100–300 tokens per second (Gemini docs), via an MCP bridge (mcp-gemini-video-understanding).

Edit footage locally; use the cloud for code

Footage belongs on your machine. Claude Code cloud sessions run on roughly 4 vCPUs, 16 GB RAM and 30 GB disk with no GPU listed. ffmpeg isn't preinstalled, foreground commands time out after 2 minutes by default (10 max), and Anthropic itself says to use Remote Control on your own hardware "for workloads beyond these limits" (Cloud environments). Code reaches the VM by cloning from GitHub. Non-GitHub bundles must be under 100 MB, and untracked files are excluded (Claude Code on the web). Multi-gigabyte raw footage doesn't fit that model.

Task Local (terminal / Desktop) Remote Control (local, steered from phone) Cloud session
Ingest, transcribe (whisper.cpp on Apple Silicon) Best Good Poor (CPU-only, disk)
ffmpeg cuts & renders of real footage Best Best for walk-away renders Poor (timeouts, 30 GB)
Resolve / Premiere MCP control Required (app must be running) Works Not possible
Remotion / HyperFrames graphics code Good Good Good (small, git-friendly)
Writing skills / CLAUDE.md style guides Good Good Good
Calling Pexels / ElevenLabs / Gemini APIs Easy Easy Needs custom network allowlist + secrets

The demo workflow worth teaching goes like this. First, transcribe with word timestamps. Second, have Claude propose a keep-list or cut list that you approve (video-use's rule is "never touch the cut without strategy approval"). Third, render with padding, 30 ms audio fades at each cut and frame-accurate re-encoding, not stream copy. Fourth, run an audio chain: high-pass, presence EQ, 3:1 compression, loudness normalization. Fifth, burn chunked captions. Sixth, add Remotion or HyperFrames overlays with a written design direction. Seventh, re-transcribe the output to confirm no words were clipped, then export XML to an NLE for a taste pass. Every correction goes into CLAUDE.md so the next video starts smarter (video-use, chrislema/videoeditor, video-editor-skills, ButterCut). The bugs worth showing on camera are the real ones people hit: clipped words from timestamp drift, pops at cuts, keyframe drift, ffmpeg builds missing drawtext/subtitles, and Claude estimating durations instead of computing them.

Where your understanding is incomplete

You've clearly done the thinking, and your instincts on trust and the course economy are good. These are the gaps that will cost you views or credibility if you don't close them.

1. Packaging comes first, not last. Your list (hook, re-hooks, visuals, sound, value) starts after the click. On YouTube the title and thumbnail decide whether anyone sees the hook. MrBeast and Colin & Samir lock the title and thumbnail before scripting (Musicbed). Write three title and thumbnail pairs per video before you film anything.

2. Retention graphs are your real editor. YouTube tells you exactly where people leave: Intro % at 30 seconds, dips, spikes and top moments (YouTube Help). Thumbnail tests pick winners by watch-time share, not CTR (YouTube Help). Plan to read your graph 48 hours after publishing and edit the next video around it.

3. Claude doesn't natively watch video or hear audio. It reads transcripts and sampled frames. If you say "Claude watched my footage," commenters who've tried it will call it out. The accurate line ("it reads your video") is also the better line. Re-check right before recording, because this area changes fast.

4. Generation and editing are different problems. The spaghetti meme tracks generative video. Claude is not a video model, and Anthropic has none. Also, Sora is gone: the app shut down Apr 26, 2026 and the API on Sep 24, 2026 (OpenAI). Don't list it as a current tool.

5. "Editing with Claude" mostly means motion graphics plus rough cuts. Even Ali's 97% was "especially on the motion graphics front." Most viral demos are code-generated animations, not edits of real footage. Make this distinction explicitly. It's one of your sharpest insights.

6. "More video watched" is a share shift, not growth. Total attention is roughly flat. Video wins by eating TV. That actually strengthens your competition argument, so use it.

7. The pyramid framing will get pushback. Fortune verified a real AdSense earner. Say "power-law plus arbitrage window, and the most reliable money is selling the dream." That's harder to dunk on. And if you ever sell a course yourself, address it head-on, because viewers will.

8. Your old formats are what the platforms are now policing. Templated data races and chat formats fall under YouTube's July 2025 "repetitive / mass-produced" policy. Even human-made faceless channels are getting hit. That's a strong personal story, not an embarrassment.

9. Don't quote guru earnings or Social Blade numbers as facts. The spread is 16x.

10. Audio beats resolution. If your budget allows one upgrade, make it the microphone and the room. There's peer-reviewed evidence for audio and none for 4K.

11. "AI" labels have a measurable engagement cost. Labeling content "AI-generated" cut emotional engagement by up to 46 points, versus about 24 for "AI-enhanced" (CBS). How you frame your own Claude-edited video matters. "I edited this with Claude, and here's what I fixed" is both more honest and more trusted.

12. Cloud Claude Code isn't built for footage. It has no ffmpeg by default, a 30 GB disk, command timeouts and GitHub-based file transfer. Edit locally, and use Remote Control if you want to walk away while it renders.

Conclusion

The best version of your video isn't "AI is overhyped" or "AI changes everything." It's that AI turned production into a commodity, and the scarce things are now attention, trust and taste. The "Claude killed video editors" genre illustrates the thesis perfectly: dozens of creators used the same cheap tools to make the same hook, some of it paid for, while demand for human editors grew. Your content advantage comes from the same thing you're arguing for. A real person with receipts and a point of view is exactly what this flood can't reproduce.

One more implication worth saying on camera: the winners of this shift may not be the people using AI best, but the people who own the relationship with the audience. That covers platforms under Aggregation Theory, personalities under the trust data, and, ironically, the course sellers. The question every creator, editor and indie developer now faces is who owns the demand, not whether they can make the thing. Answering that for yourself, on screen, is the moat.
